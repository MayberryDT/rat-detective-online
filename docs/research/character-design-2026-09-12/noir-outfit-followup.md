## Recommendation

**Use a composed, two-tone detective overcoat: one shared fedora, a contrasting collar-and-lapel treatment that continues around the back, a small shirt insert, a short tucked necktie, and color accents on the existing cuffs. Keep the rat’s anatomy and most of the coat unchanged.**

The main challenge to your proposal is this: **you could spend the entire redesign budget improving the chest while leaving the view players actually use almost unchanged.** The collar treatment, hat–coat relationship, and visible fur should carry the variation. The shirt and tie should enrich the character up close—not be expected to identify distant players.

In the [actual shoulder-camera screenshot](sandbox:/mnt/data/rat_handoff_extracted/Rat_Detective_Research_Handoff/references/current-shoulder-street.png), the back of the head, ears, hatband, collar, coat and tail are visible; the chest is not. In [03 — Composed](sandbox:/mnt/data/rat_handoff_extracted/Rat_Detective_Research_Handoff/concepts/03-composed.png), I would carry forward the closer collar and narrower lapels, **not automatically adopt the rounder shoulder bulk**. That generated study also does not yet demonstrate how a shirt or tie will fit.

Everything below is a **design recommendation**, except where explicitly identified as a historical observation or current-model evidence. Improved recognition or affection remains untested.

## 1. Recommended outfit: composed overcoat, not miniature human suit

The design should suggest **“this little rat considers himself properly dressed for serious work.”** That is the intended personality—not a claim about how children already perceive him.

| Element                                           | Decision     | Specific treatment and reason                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Small head, wedge muzzle, ears and expression** | **KEEP**     | Preserve the current head size, projecting muzzle and half-lidded eyes. Do not silently import the rounder eyes from 01 or enlarge the head to accommodate clothing.                                                                                  |
| **Shared hat**                                    | **MODIFY**   | Standardize on the current fedora’s broad, thin brim and modest crown, retaining a shallow top dent and clear ear separation. Do not adopt 02’s exaggerated angular pinch. All players share the same shape and proportions.                          |
| **Hatband**                                       | **KEEP**     | Retain one simple, uninterrupted band. It is useful from every direction; do not add a buckle, feather or badge.                                                                                                                                      |
| **Long coat**                                     | **KEEP**     | Preserve the gently tapered, continuous body. Most of the coat should remain an uninterrupted color mass, with no waist, visible legs or conventional suit torso.                                                                                     |
| **Collar and lapel shape**                        | **MODIFY**   | Move toward 03’s closer framing and narrower pointed lapels. Keep the front sufficiently open around the muzzle. Do not build out padded or rounded human shoulders beneath them.                                                                     |
| **Contrasting collar/lapels**                     | **MODIFY**   | Use one coordinated facing color across the lapel faces and the **outward-visible side and rear collar surfaces**. This must not be merely a contrasting inner lining that disappears from behind. Keep the finish matte and the contrast controlled. |
| **Small shirt opening**                           | **MODIFY**   | Add a shallow, light-colored insert between the lapels. Suggest the shirt collar with minimal edges rather than a second projecting collar. Do not open the coat down the torso or create a visible neck.                                             |
| **Tucked necktie**                                | **MODIFY**   | Add one compact knot and a short visible blade that disappears beneath the closed coat. It must read as a tie, not an isolated triangular badge. No loose end, clip, knot detail or separate motion requirement.                                      |
| **Cuff accents**                                  | **MODIFY**   | Recolor the existing cuff surfaces to repeat the collar/lapel color. Do not add another cuff, wrist strap, cufflink or projecting shirt cuff. Preserve the paw, grip and weapon/case clearances.                                                      |
| **Front closure indication**                      | **OPTIONAL** | One shallow, low-contrast overlap may help the body read as a coat. Include it only if useful in the actual model; do not add a prominent center stripe or button grid.                                                                               |
| **Additional tailoring and accessories**          | **OMIT**     | No belt, epaulettes, storm flap, rear belt, pocket flaps, waistcoat, second jacket, tie pin, gloves or boots. These would compete with the simple rat-and-coat construction rather than strengthen it.                                                |

### The important limits

**Do not make room for the tie by changing the rat.** The order of priority is: muzzle and face clearance, collar silhouette, shirt opening, then tie. If a recognizable knot and short blade cannot fit inside the existing upper-chest area, remove the tie before enlarging the head, lengthening the neck or opening the coat farther.

**Do not confuse contrast with trim everywhere.** Broadly contrasting lapels can become theatrical, especially when shiny, outlined or very bright. My recommendation is a restrained material-color change—not white piping, glossy tuxedo lapels or a decorative border around every edge. That possible “eveningwear” reading is a design risk to test, not a universal rule.

**Do not decorate the back just because it is plain.** A rear flap, belt or emblem would introduce another major feature. First use the existing visible collar, hatband, head and ears. The coat’s large quiet back helps preserve the silhouette.

The shirt and tie are worthwhile as close-view characterization. They should not force additional detail into every other part of the model.

## 2. Fixed geometry versus clothing and fur variation

**Use one shared outfit construction, curated outfit palettes, and a small vetted fur palette.** Do not turn every material region into an independent player-facing color picker.

### Recommended appearance structure

| Region                                  | Fixed across players                                  | Permitted variation                  | Coordination rule                                                                                  |
| --------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Hat crown and brim**                  | Same fedora geometry, dent, proportions and placement | One hat color                        | Crown and brim always match. Hat and coat are designed as a pair, but need not be identical.       |
| **Hatband**                             | Same width and location                               | Band color within each outfit preset | Usually supplies a lighter accent. No added band hardware.                                         |
| **Coat body**                           | Same length, taper, shoulders and hem                 | Main coat color                      | Remains the largest uninterrupted clothing region.                                                 |
| **Collar, lapels and existing cuffs**   | Same shared shapes                                    | **One linked facing color**          | These regions change together; no separate left/right lapel or cuff colors.                        |
| **Shirt insert**                        | Same small opening                                    | A restrained shirt color             | Usually a pale neutral, visibly separated from the tie.                                            |
| **Necktie**                             | Same selected geometry for everyone                   | Tie color                            | Echoes another outfit color where possible, but must remain distinct from the shirt.               |
| **Head, muzzle and ear backs**          | Same anatomy and surface treatment                    | A limited set of solid fur colors    | All these fur surfaces change together. No separate muzzle masks or patterned ear backs initially. |
| **Inner ears, paws and tail**           | Same anatomy and dusty-pink skin treatment            | None initially                       | Keep separate from the fur control. In particular, the backs of the ears remain fur-colored.       |
| **Eyes, nose, pistol and carried case** | Preserve existing identity and gameplay treatment     | Outside this outfit system           | Do not use outfit customization to recolor equipment or gameplay-state cues.                       |
| **Promotional presentation**            | Same actual costume and proportions                   | Lighting, framing and render quality | Do not add portrait-only buttons, tie details or anatomy that the playable character lacks.        |

That gives you **six clothing color regions**, but not six unrelated colors: coat, hat, band, facings/cuffs, shirt and tie.

### How the palettes should work

For each outfit, start with **two compatible clothing-color families plus a light neutral**, allowing tonal variations within them. This is an art-direction constraint, not a psychological formula.

The hat and coat can contrast meaningfully. The collar/lapels and cuffs then make that contrast feel deliberate rather than like two randomly selected garments. The shirt and tie complete the outfit without becoming additional competing focal points.

One correction to the “repeated accent” proposal: **do not automatically link the tie to the hatband.** A pale band works against a dark hat, but the same pale color may disappear on a pale shirt. Repetition is useful only when it preserves local contrast.

My proposed canonical starting palette would retain **red hat and coat, gold fur**, with muted oxblood facings/cuffs, warm cream band/shirt and a deep wine tie. That is a proposed anchor—not a final palette or a historically reconstructed costume. Other presets should explore more separation between hat and coat rather than simply recoloring this entire arrangement together.

Avoid pinstripes, tiny checks, contrasting stitching and shiny trim in the shared model. Those would add complexity without providing a dependable large-scale identification cue.

### Fur: allow it, with limits

**I recommend fur variation.** The current rear view exposes the back of the head and both ear backs, giving fur a useful visible area that the tie does not have.

Start by evaluating four solid candidates: **the existing warm gold, warm taupe, warm gray and muted ivory**. Preserve the same geometry and smooth treatment. Do not introduce realistic fur textures or markings at this stage.

Players could choose fur separately from an outfit, but only among combinations that have been checked together. Reject combinations that lose the face against the collar or collapse into the environment. Near-black and brilliant white should not be initial defaults.

This is not about producing sixteen unmistakably different uniforms. Cosmetics should provide individuality; they should not become the sole system for identifying players or understanding combat.

### Dark-setting and gameplay-color limits

Do not make every facing black merely because it is “noir.” On a dark coat in a dark street, a slightly lighter facing may work better. Establish that in the existing renderer rather than by brightening the city.

Likewise, keep cosmetic red **static and without additional glow or pulse effects**. The packet identifies existing danger/buff uses of red. A red outfit must still be distinguishable from those states through their non-cosmetic treatment. That requires a later state-on/state-off comparison; the supplied screenshots do not establish it.

## 3. Specific noir references—and what to take from them

The useful reference is **selective costume structure**, not wholesale replication of a human wardrobe. For black-and-white material, “light” and “dark” below describe visible value relationships, not inferred fabric colors.

| Reference                                                           | Sourced observation                                                                                                                                                                                                       | Translation to Rat Detective — design judgment                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The Big Sleep (1946): Philip Marlowe**                            | BFI’s greenhouse-scene material shows a light shirt and contrasting long necktie after Marlowe removes his jacket. The neckwear remains a distinct shape against the shirt. ([BFI][1])                                    | Borrow **shirt–tie separation** and the working-detective association. Compress this into a tiny insert and short tucked blade. The film is not evidence for a tucked tie or contrasting overcoat lapels.                                                                    |
| **In a Lonely Place (1950): Dixon Steele**                          | Criterion’s illustrated essay shows the screenwriter in a bow tie, light shirt and dark jacket. This is a serious noir character, not a comic detective. ([The Criterion Collection][2])                                  | A bow tie is a legitimate noir-era option, not inherently clownish. However, its horizontal wings are a poor fit for this rat’s already crowded muzzle-and-lapel area. That spatial objection matters more than a stereotype about bow ties.                                 |
| **Le Samouraï (1967): Jef Costello**                                | This later French noir uses immaculate clothing and a trench-coat collar pulled high as part of Costello’s controlled presentation. Criterion explicitly discusses that visual restraint. ([The Criterion Collection][3]) | Borrow **precise hat/collar relationships and an orderly silhouette**. Do not borrow the human tailoring, the character’s occupation or a dependence on deliberate clothing-adjustment performances. Your version must communicate composure while moving at full speed.     |
| **American Chesterfield coat, 1929–30, Metropolitan Museum of Art** | The surviving coat has a contrasting dark collar, body-colored lapels and a comparatively quiet back. The front and rear object photographs make those distinctions visible. ([The Metropolitan Museum of Art][4])        | This is the strongest period-garment reference for **a contrasted collar without decorating the entire coat**. Extend that contrast down the rat’s small lapels and onto its cuffs only as a deliberate stylized adaptation—not as a claim of exact historical construction. |

There is also a useful construction precedent: the Fitzwilliam Museum describes Chesterfields with covered front fastenings and often velvet collars. That supports simplifying the closure rather than adding a row of conspicuous buttons. It does **not** require copying velvet texture or a complete Chesterfield pattern. ([The Fitzwilliam Museum][5])

**I would call the result a noir-inspired overcoat, not a literal trench coat.** That avoids treating belts, shoulder straps and storm flaps as missing ingredients that must eventually be added.

The most important historical correction is that **contrasting collar, contrasting lapels and matching cuffs are not one inseparable classic-noir uniform**. Your proposed combination is an original cartoon simplification. Its success should be judged on this rat, not defended as mandatory authenticity.

## 4. Necktie versus the two alternatives

Choose **one neckwear treatment for the shared model**. I would not introduce all three as customization options before resolving which best serves the base character.

| Treatment                                | What it contributes                                                                                                                          | Main tradeoff                                                                                                                                                           | Recommendation                                                                                                                              |
| ---------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Short tucked necktie**                 | A compact vertical mark and a deliberately dressed appearance. My intended reading is “serious about his job,” without needing an animation. | Needs enough visible blade to register as a tie. Offers little benefit from behind or at distance.                                                                      | **Default choice.** Keep it shallow, compact and clearly separated from the shirt.                                                          |
| **No tie, small open-neck shirt insert** | The cleanest face/collar area while retaining layered clothing and coordinated colors.                                                       | Slightly less formal and less distinctive in close front views, in my judgment.                                                                                         | **Best fallback** if the tie crowds the muzzle or requires disproportionate enlargement. Keep the rest of the recommended outfit unchanged. |
| **Small bow tie**                        | A more conspicuously dapper character; historically compatible with noir, as the reference above demonstrates.                               | Adds width precisely where the snout, collar and lapels already compete. No rear-view advantage. Its intended personality may differ from “composed working detective.” | **Choose only as a deliberate character-direction change**, not because it seems automatically cuter or more child-friendly.                |

The necktie wins narrowly because it uses the available space vertically. The bow tie is not wrong because of its cultural associations; it is less suitable **for this particular body construction**.

None of these choices has demonstrated greater appeal to children yet.

## 5. Final brief and decisions

### What still needs a later test

The shoulder capture is explicitly a static review with no gameplay input, so it does not establish moving-camera readability. 

The smallest useful later comparison is the **same actual model with tucked tie versus no tie**, holding the head, hat, collar, lighting and camera constant. Check front, side, rear and shoulder views—including aiming and case-carry poses. Reject any version requiring changed muzzle clearance, grip, collision expectations or action timing.

Separately, check the darkest and lightest outfit/fur combinations in streets and sewers, including a red outfit with relevant gameplay states off and on. If those checks pass, a small audience comparison should distinguish **recognition, liking the character, and choosing to play as it**. A preferred portrait is not evidence of all three.

### Final design brief

> Evolve the actual Rat Detective model toward 03’s restrained collar and narrower lapels without importing larger shoulders or altered anatomy. Use one shared, shallow-dented fedora. Preserve the small wedge-shaped head, existing expression, long simple coat, tiny cuffs and low trailing tail. Apply one coordinated facing color to the visible rear/side collar, front lapels and existing cuffs. Add a small recessed shirt insert and compact tucked tie within the existing upper-chest space; remove the tie before changing proportions. Keep the lower coat quiet. Use curated clothing palettes and limited solid fur choices. Preserve equipment, clearances and uninterrupted control. Personality should be present in the dressed silhouette, not depend on a performance.

### Remaining decisions for you

1. **Neckwear:** approve the tucked tie as the shared default, with no tie as the space/readability fallback—or deliberately choose the bow-tie direction.
2. **Contrast strength:** approve a visibly different collar/lapel/cuff color, including the rear collar—not merely a subtle lining visible in portraits.
3. **Fur:** allow a small, vetted solid-color selection, or keep every rat gold.
4. **Canonical appearance:** retain red clothing and gold fur as the main brand representative while other players use coordinated outfit presets.

**The ingredient list is otherwise ready to settle: richer color relationships and restrained upper-coat layering, not additional accessories or a more human-shaped rat.**
