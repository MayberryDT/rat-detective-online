      Rat Detective: Character Recognition and Appeal
1. The central recommendation
Preserve the recognizable core of the existing rat, give him a distinctive way of behaving, and test
recognition separately from affection and play preference. Do not begin with a wholesale redesign,
more accessories, or a long biography.

A productive character proposition for this game is: a dead-serious little detective who refuses to lose
his dignity in a ridiculous cheese-ball world. This is a proposed creative direction, not an established
audience finding. It connects the noir costume to the comedy without requiring dialogue, cinematics, or new
investigative mechanics.

The desired response—“That’s Rat Detective; I love him; I want to play”—contains several different design
problems. Treating them as one “appeal” score can conceal the reason a design fails.

This four-part framework is a production and testing recommendation. It is not a validated psychological
scale.

Ehrenberg-Bass’s distinctive-assets framework provides a useful narrower distinction: an asset needs both
recognition and an association that points to the intended brand rather than competitors. A new character
must build those associations; an attractive design does not arrive with them automatically. 1

The practical objective is not “as famous as Mario on first exposure.” It is “easier to recognize,
remember, understand, and enjoy than the current baseline after comparable exposure.”

Scope and confidence
The visual assessment uses the earlier seven-page Rat-Detective-Visuals.pdf, especially the character
sheet on page 7 and city views on pages 2–3. It does not establish what the current executable, animations,
camera, or customization system now contain. No Rat Detective child playtest data were supplied.
Recommendations below are therefore candidates to validate, not findings that the present character is
failing.2

The evidence combines original developer accounts, a published child-avatar study, broader game-
motivation research, and explicit design judgments. None of the reviewed sources establishes a universal
combination of shapes, colors, or facial proportions that produces a successful children’s franchise.

2. What the useful comparisons actually teach
Mario: visual identity can solve a gameplay problem
Miyamoto’s account of Mario’s original design ties the cap, moustache, and clothing to severe pixel
constraints. Contrasting sleeves and overalls made arm movement easier to distinguish; the face and head
treatment reduced difficult details. These are functional design decisions that became identity cues, not
evidence that children universally prefer caps or moustaches. 3

Application: judge the rat while running, aiming, turning, and being viewed at actual play size. A beautiful
promotional pose cannot establish whether the character works in motion.

Sonic: the appearance should agree with the temperament
SEGA’s official characterization combines speed with independence, impatience, loyalty to promises, and
helping others. The useful lesson is coherence: the character’s behavior supports the energetic impression
of the design.4

Application: decide what the rat consistently notices and how he responds. “Cool, funny, brave” is not
enough to direct animation. “Suspicious glance, decisive action, exaggerated effort to look composed
afterward” is actionable.

Splatoon: start from the activity, not a detachable mascot
Nintendo’s developers describe trying different character concepts and arriving at squids because the
identity made sense with ink-based actions. The character concept and the game’s central material became
mutually reinforcing.5

Application: make the cheese-ball interaction part of the rat’s performance and visual vocabulary. A
cheese-shaped weapon already does some of that work. His aiming, recoil, reactions, and recovery can do
more without adding extra equipment. The right question is what makes this rat doing this activity different
from an arbitrary cartoon animal holding a gun.

Kirby: simplicity does not guarantee gameplay clarity
The Kirby and the Forgotten Land developers explain that Kirby’s rounded form created a particular 3D
problem: from behind, it could be difficult to tell which direction he was facing. A famous, simple character
still needed gameplay-readability work.6

Application: the coat’s broad shape may identify the rat while failing to show aim direction. Test those
separately. Passing a black-silhouette exercise is useful evidence, not a complete character-design
certificate.

Astro Bot: a face and a performance can carry identity without dialogue
Team Asobi describes Astro’s shape-changing LED eyes as central to expression and to the visual family
shared by other bots. The developer also describes his speech-like sounds as deliberately designed, rather
than treating the absence of conventional dialogue as the absence of vocal identity. 7

Minecraft and Roblox represent a different relationship
Mojang’s introduction of additional default skins explicitly expanded the ways players could represent
themselves while retaining Steve and Alex. That is not the same design goal as building affection for one
tightly authored protagonist.8

For Rat Detective, separate “I like that particular hero” from “I like being my own rat in that world.” Both are
possible. The brand can have one stable representative while playable rats retain controlled variation. A
multiplayer avatar system should not accidentally erase the character identity the marketing is trying to build.


3. Why Disney characters are more than recognizable outfits
Disney Animation describes visual development as grounded in story, emotion, composition, and the
director’s vision. Its modeling process also considers characters from different angles and treats clothing
and accessories as part of communicating personality and story. 9

That is the transferable practice: visual decisions support a recognizable person, not just a category. A
costume can establish “princess,” just as a fedora establishes “detective.” The character becomes specific
through the way that costume, body, behavior, and story work together.

Ariel as an example
Disney’s official Ariel description emphasizes curiosity and independence. In a retrospective interview, co-
director John Musker connects her red hair both to differentiation from earlier princesses and to her fiery
personality and the underwater color environment. 10

The important distinction is between remembering the red hair and having reasons to care about the
curious, determined character who has it. A color cue does the former job more directly than the latter. For
the rat, a red coat can help recognition; it cannot, by itself, communicate resourcefulness, dignity, or comic
persistence.

What not to infer from famous franchises
Recognizing a famous character is not an experiment that isolates the quality of its design. Prior familiarity,
repeated media appearances, and attachment to the experiences featuring that character are mixed
together. The existence of successful princesses therefore does not show that a particular face shape would
make a new rat equally successful.

This is a methodological caution rather than a claim that design does not matter. Use established characters
to study coherent choices. Use controlled tests of Rat Detective to decide whether a proposed change
improves this character.

Translate story into a repeatable reaction
A multiplayer character does not need a film-length character arc to have personality. The production
challenge can be much smaller: define how he responds to situations that already recur in the game.

For example, a composed detective notices a threat, acts decisively, is briefly disheveled by the result, then
corrects his hat as if nothing unusual happened. The repeated contrast is between his serious self-image

The distinction problem is specific, not a reason to start over
A red coat and broad-brimmed red hat are not unique to Rat Detective; Carmen Sandiego is a visible
example of similar costume codes.12 This does not establish copying, legal infringement, or actual confusion
among the intended players.

It does mean that “red detective outfit” is an insufficient distinctiveness claim. The rat anatomy, ear–hat
relationship, face, tail, proportions, cheese equipment, and performance should carry the specific identity
together. Test plausible near-neighbors rather than assuming the costume alone is ownable.

Preserve the setting while testing the character
The earlier city reference is intentionally very dark, with sparse illuminated windows and small pools of light.
Its local character/background contrast should be examined in moving gameplay rather than “fixed” by
replacing the noir palette. A targeted face, hat-band, or silhouette adjustment is a more discriminating first
experiment than brightening the entire world.


5. A visual system that survives real use
Choose a small set of primary cues
As a production heuristic, begin with three cue groups: the hat-and-ear arrangement; the suspicious face
emerging above the collar; and the coat-and-tail body shape. Keep the cheese pistol as a strong
supporting cue rather than the only reason the character can be identified.

The number three is not a scientific threshold. It is a constraint against adding detail before deciding what
matters. A candidate extra feature earns its place only when it improves a tested problem or supports
personality. A scar, badge, monocle, magnifying glass, necklace, and elaborate belt do not become
distinctive merely by accumulating.

Document the actual proportions and color values from the approved asset. Do not invent new
measurements from a single perspective image. The art team should decide which ratios need to remain
stable across future models and promotional illustrations.

Test more than the front view
For the intended third-person experience, treat the rear and rear-three-quarter views as primary assets.
Examine hat tilt, ear visibility, tail arc, torso lean, and the relationship between the firing arm and coat. If the
front face is rarely visible during action, give it appropriate visibility in character selection, a lobby, or an end-
of-round moment—without interrupting play simply to show it.

Valve’s Team Fortress 2 rendering paper is a useful technical precedent: its characters were designed for
rapid identification using proportion and silhouette, with rendering supporting those distinctions. It is a
readability reference, not an age-appropriateness or tone reference for this game. 13

Use a deliberately demanding review set
Review the approved candidate as a small full-body image, a cropped head, an unarmed pose, a rear view,
a flat silhouette, and a grayscale render. Then review it in the actual street and sewer lighting, with effects
and other rats present. The stylized coat must remain compatible with collision and aiming expectations;
decorative changes should not suggest a radically different vulnerable body.

These checks answer different questions. Grayscale probes dependence on hue. Silhouette probes external
form. The head crop probes the promotional icon. Busy gameplay probes recognition under competition for

A canonical red promotional rat does not require every player to remain red. Nor should red simultaneously

Make reproduction straightforward
Try a small sticker, a monochrome emblem, a simple child’s description, and a rough drawing from memory.
Use these as diagnostic translations, not proof of merchandise demand. The goal is to discover which
relationships survive simplification. Do not grade the child’s drawing skill; describing or arranging shapes is a
valid alternative.


6. Nonverbal personality and the character people control
Proposed personality brief
Role fantasy: an observant, capable rat detective caught in ludicrous combat.

Internal rule: maintain professional composure, even when the situation is visibly ridiculous.

Admired quality: decisive resourcefulness. Comic vulnerability: his dignity is easier to disturb than his
determination.

Behavioral contrast: restrained suspicion before an event; exaggerated but brief reaction to the result; an
economical return to composure.

Avoid: sadism, constant panic, empty swagger, compulsory incompetence, or a permanent grin that
discards the existing suspicious expression.

This brief is intentionally narrow enough to guide animation and open enough for the implementing artist to
discover better performances. It is not a mandate to change the approved animation feel or add a scripted
narrative.

Use the current animations as the baseline. Try one or two candidate performances first. If the player cannot
reliably read the intended attitude, simplify the acting before adding more poses.

Intention matters more than animation quantity
A useful micro-sequence is: notice something, commit to an action, respond to its consequence. The viewer
should infer what the rat was trying to do. A random assortment of dances and facial movements is not
automatically a personality.

Avoid making every action equally exaggerated. If the neutral state is already frantic, an exceptional event
has little room to register. For this proposed direction, contrast economical detective behavior with
occasional elastic reactions. The exact timing and amplitude belong to animation iteration, not an arbitrary
universal rule.

Sound can reinforce identity without words
Explore a small, consistent set of material sounds: the cheese projectile’s character, a subtle cloth response,
and perhaps a restrained nonverbal reaction. Test the same clip with and without sound. The performance
should remain intelligible when muted, and decorative audio must not conceal combat information.

Do not copy another mascot’s vocal cadence, catchphrase, or signature sound. The sound brief should
express the same competent-but-comically-dignified character as the animation, not introduce a second
personality.

Never purchase charm with worse controls
Broader game-motivation research links perceived competence and autonomy with enjoyment. It is not a
child-specific character-design formula, but it supports keeping the player’s ability to act central to the
experience.14

For this game, a mascot improvement that adds input latency, forces a pose during danger, or makes firing
less readable should be rejected even when it looks charming in a standalone clip. Personality should
decorate player agency, not compete with it.


7. One mascot, many playable rats
Choose the identity architecture explicitly
A plausible fit for this multiplayer game is one canonical brand representative plus a family of
customizable detective rats. This is a recommendation, not a claim about the currently implemented
roster.

Use the canonical rat consistently in the app icon, key art, major promotional illustrations, and introductory
materials. Let the game’s rats vary inside a recognizable design family. The same child can then recognize
“Rat Detective” and feel “this is my rat.” A separate personal name for the mascot is optional; it should solve
a real ambiguity, not add an unnecessary second name to learn.

Give the default character a complete, appealing identity. Do not deliberately make the unpaid default
embarrassing or visually inferior as a lever for children’s spending. Personalization can be expressive
without being the repair kit for an intentionally unsatisfying base design.

Build continuity across touchpoints
Keep a short reference sheet connecting the model, icon, promotional pose, expression range, and
characteristic gesture. Repeated use should reinforce the same character rather than presenting unrelated
interpretations. The logo can accompany the character while name association is being learned; an
unfamiliar mascot should not be expected to identify an unfamiliar game unaided.

Do not change the core whenever a new piece of art looks attractive in isolation. Record what changed, why
it changed, and whether testing improved. That gives future artists a rationale rather than a brittle list of
arbitrary prohibitions.


8. The audience hypothesis and the limits of the evidence
“Boys aged 8–12” is a starting hypothesis. The supplied description and comparison franchises do not
establish the eventual market. Test it rather than letting it become a permanent design restriction.

For early work, include children in both the 8–10 and 11–12 ranges, with different levels of shooter
experience. Include girls and some players outside the assumed core as comparison participants. The
purpose is not to prove that everyone likes the game; it is to notice whether the proposed audience was
unnecessarily narrow or whether different players interpret the same performance differently.

Avoid treating “for children” as “for preschoolers.” Do not assume giant eyes, baby proportions, nonstop
smiles, neon color, or gross-out humor are obligatory. Likewise, do not assume all boys want the same
aggressive fantasy. The proposed combination of competence, mischief, and recoverable embarrassment
needs testing with actual players.

Evidence that should not be overextended
Research on familiar entertainment and brand characters often uses products rather than games and much
younger children. For example, a 2024 experiment involved 66 four- and five-year-olds making object-
preference and value judgments. It cannot establish the ideal anatomy or personality for an eight-to-twelve-
year-old shooter avatar.16

Developer interviews explain design intent and production experience, not controlled causal estimates. A
successful developer choosing large eyes does not show that larger eyes improve every character. A study
of current avatar users does not show that more cosmetic options will make a new game successful.

For this project, confidence is highest in the need to test actual gameplay readability and keep a coherent
character system. Confidence is lower in any specific proposed hat angle, eye shape, gesture, palette
variation, or target demographic before testing it with Rat Detective players.

Parent and child responses are separate inputs
A child may enjoy the character while a parent dislikes the apparent violence or social environment. Test the
game’s presentation with parents as well as children; do not assume cheese weapons alone settle
suitability. This report does not determine an age rating or provide privacy or intellectual-property legal
clearance.

For testing, obtain parental permission and the child’s assent, make stopping easy, avoid unnecessary
identifying recordings, and collect only information needed for the study. Aim for voluntary enjoyment and
understandable choices, not anxiety about missing rewards or emotional pressure to keep playing.


9. A practical validation plan
The following is a proposed studio protocol. The participant counts, exposure times, and asset sizes are
practical starting points, not published universal thresholds.

Phase A: establish the current baseline
Capture the current model in front, side, rear, and three-quarter views. Record short clips of ordinary
movement, aiming, firing, and the existing success and defeat reactions. Use actual gameplay lighting and
representative visual clutter, not only studio lighting.

Record the build and asset versions. Write down the intended personality in plain language before testing so
the interpretation is not changed afterward to match whatever children say.

Start with approximately 12–18 children for qualitative problem discovery. This is not enough to declare a
statistically reliable market winner. Use a separate validation sample and a power-informed design if a later
commercial decision requires a quantified comparison.

Phase B: separate visual comprehension from learned recognition
First, briefly show the character without a label and ask what they see. Questions such as “What kind of
character is this?” and “What do you think this character does?” examine rat and detective comprehension.
Not knowing the invented name before exposure is not a failure.

Then provide the same limited, clearly named exposure to each candidate. Use the name Rat Detective
consistently. After an unrelated task, ask for an unaided description and name before showing any answer
options. Follow with a recognition lineup containing plausible alternatives: other rats, other detective outfits,
and variations of the tested character.

  Williams-Gant, Harden, and Corriveau, The influence of entertainment and brand characters on children’s object preferences and monetary

A lineup consisting of one rat, a princess, a car, and a dinosaur mostly tests broad categories. Near-
neighbors test whether the specific character was learned. Keep image size, polish, pose, background, and
exposure comparable; randomize presentation order.

Phase C: test personality without prompting the answer
Show short silent clips. Ask, “What was he trying to do?”, “How did he feel?”, and “What do you think he’ll do
next?” Avoid questions such as “Was that funny?” or “Did he look clever?” before hearing the child’s own
interpretation.

Compare the existing performance with one revised performance on the same model. Do not simultaneously
change the eyes, hat, lighting, music, and gesture, then attribute the result to animation. Test sound as a
separate addition when practical.

Phase D: test embodiment during actual play
Use a manageable play session, initially around 10–15 minutes. Observe whether the rat’s aiming and
movement are understandable, whether personality moments interrupt control, and whether the child
voluntarily chooses that character when alternatives are available.

Ask what they would tell a friend about him. Record spontaneous descriptions and meaningful points of
confusion. A laugh at one gag, or a stated intention to replay, is promising feedback—not evidence of long-
term retention.

Phase E: test delayed memory and transfer
At a follow-up roughly 24–72 hours later, ask for the character’s name and description before supplying
images. Then test recognition from another angle or an allowed costume variation. A description, sketch, or
simple shape arrangement can reveal remembered features without making artistic skill the scoring criterion.

Earlier recognition lineups can teach features. Keep procedures consistent, and use separate groups when
a clean delayed-memory estimate matters. Treat each child as a participant; repeated trials from the same
child are not independent children.

10. Production priorities and handoff
First: establish what must remain recognizable
Create a compact character sheet with approved front, side, rear, and three-quarter views; actual palette
values; critical proportions; the eye-expression range; the unarmed silhouette; and the characteristic hat–ear
and coat–tail relationships. Add a brief explanation of why each protected feature matters.

Perform a readability pass in the current game before committing to new anatomy. The first experiment
should be the smallest change that could resolve the observed issue: a pose adjustment, a visible patch of
face, ear separation, or local value contrast may be enough.

Second: prototype one signature reaction
Test a single dignity-restoring gesture and a single readable shift from suspicion to intent. Keep them
interruptible where appropriate. Compare them against the approved baseline with the same movement and
firing behavior. Expand the performance vocabulary only when the first examples communicate the intended
character.

Third: carry the result into brand and avatar rules
Choose the canonical promotional rat, define what playable variants may change, and update the icon and
key art from the approved system. Verify the result without the weapon and outside the full-body hero pose.
Do not begin with a large skin catalog, elaborate backstory, or voice script.

Brief for the implementing team
Objective: make Rat Detective easier to recognize, remember, and enjoy as a character while preserving
the established noir style and approved game feel.

Proposed creative center: a serious, observant little detective trying to remain composed in an absurd
cheese-ball fight. Favor competence with comic vulnerability over helplessness or cruelty.

Protected starting material: the existing rat anatomy, red-hat/coat identity, visible ears, suspicious face,
tail, cheese equipment, and the dark city’s visual language. These are a baseline to assess, not a ban on
evidence-led changes.

Freedom: propose alternate visual and performance solutions when they better express the same intent. Do
not treat this report’s suggested gestures, three cue groups, test timings, or avatar architecture as
mandatory implementation details.

Acceptance evidence: comparisons at actual play size and camera angles; comprehensible aim and
motion; personality understood without dialogue; no added control delay; consistent identity across
approved variants; and child feedback that distinguishes recognition from affection and willingness to play.

Decision boundary: no wholesale redesign without evidence that smaller changes cannot solve the
problem. No expansion of mechanics, lore, or monetization is required by this character brief.

Bottom line: children should be able to recognize this rat without his pistol, understand his attitude without
dialogue, and enjoy controlling him without his personality getting in the way.
