# Lag research — 2 October 2026

Plan item: [Lag research](../juice-plan.md#clarity-batch-agreed-2-october-protocol-29) in the clarity batch (branch `clarity`, protocol 29). Measured on Veelox (Intel Core Ultra 7 155H, Node 26) while other agents were working on the machine (load average 4–7), so single tick timings carry noise; byte counts and hashes are exact.

## Summary

- **The server tick is not the cause.** With 10 rats, 4 receiving clients, weapons, traps, deaths and each incident held active, a tick takes 2–3.2 ms (median), 3.7–6.5 ms (p95) and at most 17–27 ms, against a 33 ms budget.
- **The wire was heavy.** Each client was sent 144–167 KB/s in ordinary play and **391 KB/s in Cheddar Shower**, with up to 40 KB in one tick. Cheddar Shower's meteor bursts fill the 256-ball cap, and ball motion is most of the frame.
- **Fixed, losslessly:** the chaos frames are 39–47% smaller and everything a client receives is 32–40% smaller (Cheddar Shower 391 → 234 KB/s). Every bot pose, every ball position and every state the client decodes is unchanged.
- **The ping spikes did not line up** with deaths, the round reset or slow frames on the player's machine, and no two players spiked at the same time. A spike of a second or more needs the player's connection to stall. A smaller wire shortens the wait behind queued frames, but it cannot prevent a stall.
- **Trey's 43 fps is his own machine's rendering**, which is the same in every incident. Decoding a frame costs 0.2–0.4 ms.

## How bytes become ping

Every server message is reliable and ordered, and the client acknowledges it. Up to 8 chaos frames wait unacknowledged, with all other messages beside them. A pong waits behind whatever is already in the connection, so when a player's link slows, the wait is the bytes in flight divided by what the link still carries. Eight Cheddar Shower frames were about 100 KB, or 300 KB at the burst peak. The 8-frame window, not the bytes, decides when frames start being skipped. [INFERENCE] This explains why spikes follow busy moments on one connection at a time. A 2.8 s spike still needs the link to fall below about 40–100 KB/s for that time, for example a Wi-Fi stall or TCP recovering lost packets.

## What changed

All in `src/shared/chaosWire.ts`. The wire mode stays `compact-v2`.

- **Ball motion is predicted.** Once a ball has two rows, the next row carries only the miss of a straight-line prediction from them (`motionEncoding` `predict-v1`, was `delta-v1`). For a ball in flight, that is gravity's pull on its height plus a rounding unit here and there.
- **Shorter motion rows.** Numbers that are likely zero go last and trailing zeros are left off.
- **Ball handles are reused.** A handle stays as short as the most balls ever in flight at once. Before, it grew all session, to five or six digits over a long round (about 100,000 balls in the playtest's 52 minutes, counting death bursts) [INFERENCE].
- **Lists send only changes.** Corpses, laser beams, traps and meteors travel as the changed and new items plus the ids gone (`{put,drop}`), when that is shorter than the whole list. Before, one tumbling corpse resent all 16 corpses and every new laser beam resent all 16 beams. A list that changes order travels whole.
- **Impacts are rows**, without key names.

Nothing else moved:

- **No change to the server, the simulation or what the client sees.** The cadence, keyframes, the 8-frame window and the validation of every decoded frame are unchanged.
- **Bot poses and ball positions:** the trajectory hash is identical in every incident (2-minute and 10-minute runs).
- **What the client decodes:** a digest of every decoded state (canonical JSON, 3,600 frames per incident) is identical between the old and new decoders for all eight incidents.
- **Server side:** a Mousetrap press that cannot be set down rebuilds a full snapshot for its rejection. It was measured as rare (3 in 2 minutes) and left alone.

## Remaining (not changed)

- **Movement frames are about 36 KB/s per client.** They are exact doubles: the September network audit chose "no movement quantization". Rounding positions to 0.1 mm and rotations to 1e-5 would roughly halve them [INFERENCE], but it changes precision, so it is Tyler's call.
- **Burst balls.** Cheddar Shower and deaths fill the 256-ball cap (120 balls a death burst). Fewer burst balls would cut the wire further, but it changes play. It fits the open Cheddar Shower note: "consider … fewer meteors".
- **The in-flight window counts frames (8), not bytes.** A byte limit would bound the wait behind queued frames on a slow link, at the cost of skipping frames sooner. It is untested.
- **Keyframes** every 300 frames resend every ball (about 27 KB at 256 balls) and set the maximum.
- **Trey's frame rate is rendering work on his CPU:** 25 ms of main-thread work on a median frame. Auto quality kept him at auto-high at 43 fps; whether it should step down when the main thread is the limit belongs to the visual budgets (CalmMix). No browser profile was taken here.
- **Not measured:**
  - the hosted Worker;
  - storage writes holding outgoing messages (every hit forces a player write);
  - socket costs;
  - a real four-human session.
- **Pressure Surge's `pressure` field** (560 bytes a tick, filling levels) is unchanged.
- **Nothing is visible or audible.** The client decodes the same states, so there are no screenshots, and no sound changed.
